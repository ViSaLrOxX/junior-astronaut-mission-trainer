# Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer

A small browser game about running a Moon or Mars base, played from a first-person cockpit: you look out through the windscreen at drifting stars while the ship's consoles hold the controls. You pick a site and a difficulty, then keep a crew alive sol by sol: power, oxygen, water, food, shielding and morale all pull against each other. Panels are translucent dark glass with thin teal edges, buttons are tactile ship controls with clear hover, focus, pressed and disabled states, and a brief launch transition plays when a mission starts. High-contrast mode makes every panel opaque, reduced-motion keeps the scene static, and instrument animation pauses when the tab is hidden.

## Running it

Open index.html in any browser. No build step, no server, no network.

First-timers get a short guided tour that teaches by doing: you press Launch yourself, open the Status board and the Build yard yourself, live your first sol yourself, and tap the counter buttons to see where more of each comes from. Finish it and Mission Control tells you that you've learnt the basics and you're good to go. It can be skipped and reopened any time with the How to play button. Once the tour is done, the + buttons next to the Parts and Data counters open a full guided walkthrough: pick any way to earn more and it teaches you first, then takes you there. For quizzes it shows you the fact behind the next question before you answer it, so you can actually get them right.

The game is split into three rooms, switched from the top: Mission, Learn & play, and Reports. Each room opens as a menu of cards, and each card opens one dedicated workbench: the status board, the build yard, research, crew, expeditions, the log, quizzes, drills, the daily briefing, the passport, charts, briefings and downloads. One thing fills the screen at a time, and a Back to menu button returns to the cards. If a workbench sits untouched for 10 minutes it steps back to the menu on its own; the Menu timeout control next to the theme picker shortens that to 5 minutes or switches it off, and whichever workbench you had open is remembered if the page reloads, so you land back where you were. Pressing Advance one sol brings up the status board so you can watch the gauges move, and when Mission Control calls with a quiz, it opens on its own. Every quiz answer gets instant feedback: a green flash when right, a shake and the correct answer when wrong, and a short fact to read. Quiet generated sounds play on answers, with a Sound toggle at the top to mute them. A background ambience plays underneath, all synthesised in the browser with no audio files: Deep space is the default, a slow airy pad; Ship hum is a steady cabin tone; Dark matter sits lower and darker. All three are smooth beds with no beat or pulse, and the level is set with the volume slider. The knowledge passport now asks each fact as a real question and reveals the fact once answered. Workbenches and rooms fade in gently, bars ease as values change, and motion stays off for anyone with reduced-motion turned on. Data and parts counters sit at the top of the screen at all times, flashing when you earn more, each with a + button that jumps straight to every way to earn more. Sound, ambience, volume, theme, menu timeout and high contrast live together at the top right. The dense analysis panels only exist for Commander-class missions; easier classes never see the switch. The outpost view redraws smoothly with a proper sky, lit domes, blinking beacons, walking crew and drifting dust. To run the logic tests: node test.js.

## What's inside

Two real sites (lunar south pole, Jezero rim) with different radiation, ice and storm profiles. Nineteen building types, six named crew with roles and XP, random mission events drawn from a fixed-seed deck so runs are reproducible, and twenty achievements.

A story runs through each mission: engineer Ana, gardener Mei, Ravi and medic Kofi. Chapter one asks for your first build (solar array or ice drill, costs shown up front); chapter two brings a space-weather alert and a choice between shelter and research; chapter three is the quiet storm camp, dark but for one green habitat light, with an emergency reserve route if you were not ready; chapter four is the all-clear and the seedling's first new leaf. There are three endings: a thriving camp, a safe return (a valid ending, not a failure) and a difficult return that shows the warning you missed. Mission Control's last word: your mission wasn't to do everything, it was to decide what mattered.

The first sols are deliberately gentle: you start with three gauges and a few blueprints, and consoles unlock as the mission settles in (ops at sol 2, research at sol 3, crew at sol 4, expeditions at sol 6). The outpost card shows your base as a little animated scene: domes, solar panels and walking astronauts, with smoke and flicker when something is damaged. Each run also carries one scripted story beat: Earth warns of an incoming storm, the radio delay means the choice is yours, and a short debrief question afterwards asks about what just happened. When the mission ends, win or lose, a debrief lists what you learned. A Sources card in Learn & play says plainly which facts are real NASA history (MOXIE, Apollo 13, the storm that killed Opportunity in 2018, the 1989 Quebec blackout) and which numbers are simulated for learning; saved data never leaves the browser's local storage.

A workbench panel turns the live mission into readable tables: crew shape, resource runways, data pace, power margin, danger score and win odds, plus CSV and markdown exports of the whole run.

Parts keep the whole economy moving, and the Build bench lists every way to get more: a Regolith Drill digs regolith and a Regolith Workshop sinters every 10 into parts each sol, Debris Salvage expeditions bring back 12, resupply pods land more on a parts manifest, correct quiz answers pay parts and data, and data milestones bring backer shipments. The consoles are yours to restyle: a Theme picker in the top bar repaints every control (telemetry teal, amber ops, deep violet or crimson watch), and each kind of control keeps its own shape - chamfered primary keys, tabbed room switches, lettered quiz options, pill-shaped report buttons and a dashed ghost for going back.

Alongside the sim there is a quiz deck about the Moon and Mars, a drill mode that tests you on the event deck, a daily briefing card with a streak counter, a knowledge passport that tracks which facts you have mastered, a twenty-question quiz marathon with lives, an event timeline you can filter by site, and an achievement checklist that reads your unlocked set from local storage. Three more trainers round it out: Radio delay shows how long a message really takes to the Moon or Mars and lets you send a ping and wait out the trip; Pack the EVA bag is a mass-budget challenge across three spacewalk tasks where forgetting the oxygen tank or the tether ends the run before it starts; Space weights turns your Earth weight into Moon, Mars and Jupiter numbers, with the ISS reminding you that orbit means floating. Four more trainers sit beside them: Site scout asks you to pick the right ground for three mission goals (ice, ancient life, safety) from real candidates like Shackleton and Jezero, with the tradeoffs explained; Crew day planner gives you one sol to schedule under real astronaut rules - 8 hours of sleep, at least 2 of exercise, maintenance time, and no more than 11 hours of work; Docking approach puts you 300 metres out at 22 m/s with four burns to dock at 2 m/s or less; Build the rocket has you stack the stages of Saturn V, SLS and Falcon 9 in the right order, booster first. Four more sit next to those: Delta-v budget fuels three real missions (a station reboost, a Moon landing, a Mars transfer) with the rocket equation doing the maths live as you load propellant; Launch window asks when and why Earth-to-Mars windows open every 26 months; Blackout planner has you pick exactly what a Mars team must finish before solar conjunction cuts the radio for two weeks; Power budget sizes a solar array and battery bank to get a base through the night within a launch-mass limit. The life-support and flight-dynamics set adds Oxygen garden (stored air versus algae tanks for a crewed trip), Radiation shield (stack polyethylene, water, aluminium and regolith to pull a dose under the limit in a mass budget), Spin gravity (radius and spin rate for Mars or Earth gravity) and Orbit match (pick the altitude that gives the period a job needs, by Kepler's third law). Two of the newest tie straight into the game: Know your base asks five questions about the mission you are actually running - power margin, first resource to run dry, crew on their feet, morale band, sols left - and Stow the lander has you split cargo between port and starboard bays until the trim sits inside a few percent. The Learn and Play menu groups all of this under Your mission, Fly there, Live off-world and Learn the facts so nobody has to scroll one long list. The sim itself runs deeper than the trainers: every astronaut is dealt a trait at the start of a mission (Green thumb boosts greenhouse food, Tinkerer makes repairs cheaper, Optimist recovers morale faster, Pathfinder hauls more science home from expeditions), so the same base plays differently with a different crew; Mission Control talks to you: every sol carries a directive, a small optional goal like keeping every building powered or banking 8 data, with a small reward when you meet it, and five times over a mission a captain's call interrupts - a solar flare, a dust devil, a tired astronaut, a school broadcast, an ice pocket, a greenhouse pest - where you pick one of two real tradeoffs and live with it. The crew talk too: one of them says something in the log every sol, and what they say follows their trait and how the mission is treating them. Pressing Launch earns its moment: a short animated countdown with a rising rocket, a progress bar and stage callouts, skippable with a tap. The guided tour now talks like a person - short, friendly steps - with Sprout, a little rover, riding along in the corner, and a Voice button that reads every step and story card aloud using the device's own speech (tap it to hush). Story messages no longer arrive as walls of text: they open one short layer at a time, with a Keep reading tap for the next, and the answers only appear once you've read it all. Questions and answers in the Learn & Play room are set clearly larger too. The log also closes each sol with a wrap line - what the sol did to your air, water, food, parts and data, good or bad - and when Mission Control's schedule has a storm, flare, micrometeorite or quake coming in the next ten sols, an outlook line on the mission view warns you which sol it lands, so you can plan around it. In the Reports room, Postcards collects short notes from the crew as the mission reaches new sols, with a last word from Mission Control at the end.

## Data

Site parameters, events and quiz facts are baked into engine.js as plain data tables. Nothing is fetched at runtime.

Real NASA data used:
- NASA DONKI (ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI/): a saved copy of the real May 2024 space-weather records (CMEs, solar energetic particle events, geomagnetic storms), shown in the Moon story's Sources card. Saved copy, not a live feed.
- NASA Curiosity RAD: the Mars radiation scale of about 0.64 mSv per day (Hassler et al., Science 2014).
- NASA MOXIE on Perseverance (NASA JPL): the O2 Generator is named after it.
- NASA GISS Mars24: a Mars sol is 24 h 39 min 35 s.
- NASA OCHMO radiation brief and NASA NTRS EVA references (ISS suit 7 h breakdown with 30 min reserve, Artemis suit 8 h with 1 h contingency, 2 km/h walk-back limit, EVA buddy pairs): the EVA planner's reserve rules.
- Not NASA, said so in-game: the Moon radiation scale of about 1.44 mSv per day is from the Chang'e-4 LND instrument (CNSA lander, DLR instrument; Zhang et al., Science Advances 2020).
Every other number in the game is an invented teaching model.

## Merged build: Walk the base + RIM CAMP story

This build merges two lines of the game: the v31 build you sent (rooms, guided tour, HUD, themes, ambience modes, outpost scene, parts help, Sources card) and the earlier RIM CAMP build (story layer, saved NASA DONKI record, eerie ambience, supply boost, decision-linked learning). Nothing from either was dropped except where noted below.

**New: Walk the base** (first card in the Mission room, opens by itself after Launch and after each Advance one sol, unless the tour is running). You are the astronaut. Walk along the base with the arrow keys or A and D, the on-screen buttons, or by tapping a job in Today's jobs. Press E or Do job here next to a module:
- Routine jobs (water the plants, swap the air filter, empty the ice hopper, brush the solar panels, tidy the bunks, log a sample) give small bounded rewards, once per module per sol. Air, water or food jobs pay double when that gauge is below 40.
- Faults: a damaged module smokes and flashes. Patching it by hand costs 2 hours and 3 to 5 parts (the engine's normal repair cost).
- The mission: the Mission console runs science experiments (3 hours each, two per sol, about 0.9 of the science per sol needed to hit the goal). The return window is the mission's sol limit: reach the science goal before it closes.
- Time: each sol gives 8 working hours (6 during a storm). Jobs cost 1 hour, faults 2, experiments 3. Unused hours are lost when you advance the sol.
- Storms: dust blows across the scene, the sky darkens and outdoor jobs are blocked. A banner warns when a storm is forecast for next sol.
- Other crew walk between modules as scenery. Machinery moves (solar tilt, dish, drill, plants, status lights). Reduced-motion turns off bobbing, dust and easing but everything stays playable. Jobs are also a plain list with buttons for keyboard and screen-reader users.
All of this is a simplified teaching model, not real procedures.

## First-person view

Walk the base is now seen through the astronaut's helmet. It is software 3D drawn on a 2D canvas (no WebGL, no libraries, still one folder you open in a browser): perspective projection, shaded faces, a low sun with long shadows, craters and rocks, distance haze, domes, solar arrays, comms dishes, drills and a mission console built as simple 3D shapes, other crew walking, a visor with readouts, gloves that move when you work, dust and darkening in storms. Controls: left/right arrows or A/D turn, up/down or W/S walk, E does the job you face, or tap a job to be walked there. On-screen buttons do the same on phones. The jobs, hours, return window and storm rules are unchanged. It is more realistic than the cartoon side view but it is low-poly, not photorealistic: no textures or true lighting. That needs WebGL (for example three.js) and real 3D models, which is a bigger step.

## Music (Dark shanty)
The default ambience is "Dark shanty": an original D-minor slow 3/4 bass march at 62 bpm (low kick, choir-like pad, drone, sea swell), written for a deep, ominous sea-shanty mood. It is NOT "Hoist the Colours": no melody, lyrics or recording from that song is used. Change it in the ambience select (Eerie ship, Deep space, Ship hum, Dark matter are still there). Audio was only measured (offline render, peak -0.9 dB, strong low-frequency energy, no page errors), not listened to by the builder. audio/shanty-preview.mp3 is an offline render of the same generator.

## Science notebook, captain's log and mission patch

Added in lab.js (new card "Science notebook" in the Mission room). The engine rules are unchanged except one small once-only reward.
- **Hypothesis and readings.** Hypothesis: a shielded habitat gives a lower daily dose than standing outside. Every experiment you run at the Mission console also takes one simulated dosimeter reading, outside or inside as you choose. The readings are invented for teaching: they are scattered around a scale value by a made-up 20 percent.
- **Scale values (dose equivalent, mSv per day) and where they come from.** Mars: 0.64, measured on the surface by the Curiosity rover's RAD instrument in Gale crater (7 Aug 2012 to 1 Jun 2013, solar maximum). The quoted +/-0.12 is measurement uncertainty, not day-to-day variation. Moon: about 1.44 (60 microSv per hour x 24), measured by the Chang'e-4 lander's LND instrument in von Karman crater on the far side in 2019. That is not the south pole, and Jezero rim is not Gale crater. They are scales for fictional bases, not predictions for those places. Instruments measure absorbed dose (mGy); dose equivalent is derived with a quality factor.
- **Conclusion.** After 3 readings in each place you conclude "shielding lowers the dose" or "no clear drop yet". The game compares your call with a simple game rule: the gap must be more than 2 times the combined standard error of your readings. That is a teaching heuristic, not a validated statistical test. With no shielding built, the rule says "no clear drop". A call that matches the rule pays +6 science and +8 parts once.
- **Crew dose ledger.** A teaching tally: each sol every crew member adds the scale dose cut by a game shielding rule (1 / (1 + 0.5 x shield level)). Crew on expeditions get the full amount. It is not a health calculation, and NASA's 600 mSv career standard is mentioned for scale only, not as a safety meter. Solar particle events are not counted.
- **Mission brief.** Goal, return window and hours per sol in one place.
- **Captain's log, Back on Earth, mission patch.** The end debrief adds a first-person log built from your run, short sourced notes (the dose instruments, a Chang'e-4 team comment on covering a habitat with lunar soil, and MOXIE's 122 g of oxygen over 16 runs) and a mission patch you can download as a PNG.
- **Sources (links also in the notebook):** Hassler et al. 2014 (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1244797), Zhang et al. 2020 (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaz1334), Kiel University (https://www.uni-kiel.de/en/research/details/news/203-moon-mission), NASA OCHMO brief (https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/radiation-protection-technical-brief-ochmo.pdf), NASA JPL MOXIE (https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-oxygen-generating-experiment-moxie-completes-mission/). The 1.44 value uses the Kiel/CSSAR "about 60 microSv per hour"; I read the paper's abstract (absorbed dose 13.2 +/- 1 microGy per hour) but not its full text.

Honest limits. The scatter, the shielding factor and the conclusion rule are game rules. The DONKI record is a saved real past event, not live and not a lunar storm simulation. The Rim Camp storm is invented. Story pop-ups are still modal. Not tested on a real phone or Safari. Not hosted, submitted or entered anywhere, and nothing here claims to win any category.

## Realism build: EVA planner, radiation survey, hands-on repair, radio calls

New file survey.js; changes in walk.js, lab.js, story.js, story.css.

- **EVA planner.** Any outdoor job (the Solar Array and other outside modules) and every survey reading now opens a plan before you go: egress 0.25 h + walk out + task + walk back + ingress 0.25 h, with a 1 h suit reserve and a walk-back check. Walking is 2 km/h. You pick an EVA buddy (EVAs are done in pairs, so at least 2 crew must be home and well). The plan is refused if the walk back is longer than the game's own cap of 1 h (an invented, conservative rule; NASA plans real range around the walk back, but the 1 h figure is not a NASA number), if the total plus reserve is over 8 h, or if you do not have the hours left.
  Sources: ISS suit, 7 h maximum = 15 min egress + 6 h work + 15 min ingress + 30 min reserve (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140000238/downloads/20140000238.pdf). Artemis suit, at least 8 h, nominal 6 +/- 2 h, 1 h contingency, 2 km/h walking, distance limited by walking back (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210026296/downloads/Scoville%20Final%20presentation%20-%20compressed.pptx.pdf). Buddy pairs (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230008183/downloads/Tenets%20of%20Lunar%20EVA%20for%20Artemis%20III_Public%20Release.pdf). Mars sol = 24 h 39 min 35 s (https://www.giss.nasa.gov/tools/mars24/help/notes.html).
- **Radiation survey.** Five survey stakes stand on a fictional map. Walk to one, face it, press E, plan the EVA, and take a detector reading. Each spot has a hidden, invented dose factor, and readings are noisy. After readings at 3 different spots you can spend 6 parts on an emergency shelter at one of them. On storm sols and solar-event sols a shelter at a lower-dose spot gives a small health refund. The debrief says where you put it and what the best spot within walk-back range was. The far stake is beyond the walk-back limit on purpose.
- **EVA dose.** Hours outside add dose in the ledger without counting the day twice: you get the full outside dose for each EVA hour (separate row, not in the daily tally), your buddy gets only the extra over the habitat's shielded rate. Each of you takes the full outside dose, it is not split. The buddy list holds only crew who are alive and at home, so it can never be you or someone away.
- **Hands-on repair.** Fixing an outdoor fault is a 3-step timing task (press E or click when the marker is in the green). Each hit beyond the first saves a part.
- **Radio calls.** While Walk the base is open, story cards appear inline in the walk panel under the controls as "RADIO CALL" (aria-modal off, focus not taken), so they do not cover the view, the buttons or the footer. Decisions still need an answer. Outside Walk the base, cards are still modal pop-ups.
- **Limits.** All distances, hours, dose factors and the map are invented. The planner is not a validated EVA timeline or training simulator and does not predict anything about a real site. Test mode (`__walkFast`) skips the planner and repair game; `__evaReal` keeps the planner costs. Not tested on a real phone or Safari. Story cards are docked, not removed, and still carry aria-modal.

- **Other fixes in this build.** The decorative cockpit frame sits behind the content now, so the footer no longer overlays buttons or the notebook when scrolling. Walking to a target now steers around modules instead of getting stuck on the Mission console.

## Mission build: suit supplies, return decision, fault diagnosis, Mission Control

New file suit.js (loaded after survey.js); hooks in walk.js and lab.js. One connected mission: survey with the detector, watch your suit, diagnose what breaks, decide whether to finish or turn back, report to Mission Control, and see it all in the debrief.

- **Suit supplies.** The suit has three supplies: oxygen 6 h, battery 5.5 h, CO2 filter 5 h. These capacities, the rates (repairs use more) and the 0.5 h reserve are INVENTED for the game. Real suits do track oxygen, power and CO2 removal, and the real reserves in the sources above are time based too, but these numbers are not from any suit. The EVA plan shows the projected use and what is left. A sortie that would leave less than the reserve is refused. After each EVA the suit is refilled from the base: oxygen and parts (a fresh CO2 filter), so exploring has a real cost to the base.
- **Return-to-base decision.** Halfway through a task a suit warning may hit (CO2 filter loading, oxygen leak, battery cell dropout; invented events, more likely on long sorties). A card shows your readout if you finish the task and if you abort now. Finishing into the reserve leaves your buddy shaken (fatigue and morale). Running short means an emergency walk back: your buddy loses 12 health and the rest of the day is lost. Aborting costs the task and the hours and is the lower-risk option inside the game (supplies stay above reserve). That is a game rule, not a statement about real safety. Planning a repair budgets one extra hour for a wrong diagnosis in both the hours and the suit supplies, so a misdiagnosis cannot push you past the reserve after you start. You cannot walk off, start another job, radio in or end the day while a sortie is under way.
- **Diagnosis.** Outdoor faults start with a diagnosis: three instrument readings, three possible causes (invented faults, matched to the equipment you are at: power for the solar array, dish for comms, heat for habitat and life-support modules). The right cause widens the repair timing window. A wrong one costs 1 extra hour and 1 part. Then the hands-on repair follows as before.
- **Mission Control.** A current objective is always shown in the walk panel (first: take a reading at the plain stake). "Radio Mission Control" (0.25 h) reports what you pick as the lead finding and gives a revised objective. Each completed objective pays +4 science once. The control room, its replies and the crew are fictional, and the game answers at once. Real delays depend on distance (up to about 22 minutes one way at Mars, NASA NTRS 20220013418).
- **Debrief.** The captain's log now says how many check-ins, diagnoses (right and wrong) and suit warnings you had, and whether you aborted, pushed on, or ran short.
- **Limits.** All suit numbers, events, faults and replies are made up and not a real procedure or safety threshold. The planner, suit and events are skipped in test mode (`__walkFast`), and `__evaReal` plus `__walkFast` auto-picks the safer option. `__suitForce` forces an event for testing. Not tested on a real phone or Safari.

## Walk update: jetpack sprint, low-gravity hop and a livelier HUD

Walking the base is quicker and louder now. Hold Shift (or the Boost button) for a jetpack sprint: speed roughly doubles while the boost meter lasts, and it refills when you stop sprinting. Press Space away from a module for a low-gravity hop, higher on the Moon than on Mars. Doing a job pops the reward up on screen, a welding spark shows while you patch a fault, and a strip along the top marks every module so you can see what is ahead, with damaged ones in red and the mission console in blue. The visor readout now shows boost, hours, sol and science while you walk. Story cards can also be read aloud in a friendlier voice: the game picks a clear English voice from whatever the device offers instead of the robotic default.

All of it is still a teaching toy: real suits do not have jetpacks, and a real hop on the Moon needs both legs and some nerve.
